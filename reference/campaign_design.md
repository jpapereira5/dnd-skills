# Campaign Design Tenets

## What This Document Is

This is the structural blueprint for how João designs D&D campaigns. It defines WHAT gets built — the architectural principles that govern every campaign, quest, faction, NPC, and choice — regardless of setting, level range, or narrative voice.

**This is NOT DM style.** DM style (see `dm_profile.md`) defines HOW content is delivered at the table — narration voice, pacing, sensory language, disclosure techniques. Campaign design defines what that voice is delivering.

**Think of it as:** DM style is your voice. Campaign design is your skeleton.

---

## The Core Thesis

**Everything exists to manufacture impossible choices.**

Every system in this document — grey morals, the living world, discovery, trust mechanics, character revelation, intra-party tension — is INFRASTRUCTURE. These systems exist to make impossible choices land harder, feel more personal, and echo longer.

The hierarchy:
1. What makes a choice impossible? → **Tenet 1**
2. What creates the CONDITIONS for impossible choices? → **Tenets 2, 3, 4**
3. What amplifies the WEIGHT of impossible choices? → **Tenets 5, 6**
4. What generates the most PERSONAL impossible choices? → **Tenet 7**

**Source synthesis:** These tenets are derived from analyzing what works best in the Mass Effect Trilogy and Baldur's Gate 3, adapted for tabletop RPG play where player characters replace video game companions as the primary agents of drama.

## The 7 Core Tenets

---

### TENET 1: THE IMPOSSIBLE CHOICE

*The core. Everything else serves this.*

#### Definition

An impossible choice is a moment where players must decide between two or more options that are:
- **Defensible** — each option can be argued as "the right thing to do"
- **Mutually exclusive** — choosing one forecloses the other permanently
- **Costly** — every option sacrifices something real (a relationship, a resource, a belief, a person)
- **Legible** — the costs are visible BEFORE the decision, not revealed after as a gotcha

What makes a choice TRULY impossible: it is simultaneously **personal** (it affects someone the players care about) AND **political** (it reshapes the world beyond the immediate situation). You cannot separate "what's right for me" from "what's right for everyone," because they point in different directions.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

Without impossible choices, campaigns become puzzle-solving (find the right answer) or power fantasies (become strong enough to brute-force everything). Neither produces the emotional weight that makes sessions memorable years later.

The impossible choice is what players TALK about between sessions. It's what they argue about at the table. It's what they remember a year from now. Everything else is setup.

#### How to Implement

**Design Rules:**
- Every major quest must contain at least ONE moment where the party chooses between two defensible options
- The costs of each option must be communicated BEFORE the decision point — through NPC dialogue, environmental clues, or direct observation
- Neither option should be clearly superior. If one option is obviously better, it's not an impossible choice — it's a puzzle with extra steps
- The choice should split the table's opinion. If everyone immediately agrees, the choice wasn't designed well enough
- After the choice is made, the world must CHANGE visibly. The unchosen path must have real consequences that the party witnesses or hears about later

**Types of Impossible Choice:**

| Type | Structure | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Value Conflict | Two goods that exclude each other | Save the hostages OR stop the ritual (can't do both in time) |
| Loyalty Conflict | Two people/factions the party cares about, on opposite sides | Your mentor asks you to betray your ally |
| Method Conflict | Same goal, incompatible approaches — must commit to one | Negotiate peace (slow, uncertain) OR preemptive strike (fast, irreversible) |
| Sacrifice Conflict | Success requires giving up something precious | The artifact that can save the city requires a willing soul |
| Knowledge Conflict | Now that you KNOW, you can't unknow — and knowing forces action | You learn your ally committed atrocities. They don't know you know. What now? |

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — The Genophage Cure:**
Cure the genophage (risk Krogan aggression in 100 years, honor Mordin's sacrifice) or sabotage it (betray an entire species to ensure short-term galactic stability). Both are defensible. Both cost something enormous. The player knows what they're choosing.

**Mass Effect — Virmire:**
Kaidan or Ashley. You cannot save both. No trick. No creative solution. Choose.

**BG3 — The Emperor:**
Trust an entity that has been genuinely helpful but is fundamentally self-interested, or reject it and lose your primary defense against the Absolute. Neither is wrong. Both are terrifying.

**BG3 — Gale's Orb:**
Let Gale sacrifice himself to destroy the orb (lose a friend, guaranteed solution) or seek another way (keep your friend, risk everything on an uncertain path).

#### D&D Translation

At a TTRPG table, impossible choices are MORE powerful than in video games because:
- Players have built these characters over months/years — the investment is real
- There's no reload. The choice is permanent in a way video games can't achieve
- Other players at the table REACT. Social pressure exists. The group might disagree
- The DM can adapt consequences in real time — the world's response is genuinely alive

**Prep checkpoint:** Before every session, ask: "Where is the impossible choice this session?" If there isn't one, the session is setup for a future one — and that's fine. But know which it is.

### TENET 2: GREY MORALS

*Why choices are impossible — because there is no clean answer.*

#### Definition

The moral landscape of the campaign is a field of competing GOODS, not good versus evil. No faction is purely right. No antagonist is purely wrong. Every side operates from comprehensible motivations and genuine grievances. Helping one side genuinely harms another — not because one is evil, but because their needs are structurally incompatible.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

Grey morals are what MAKE impossible choices impossible. If there's a clearly evil side, the choice is easy: oppose evil. That's not a choice — it's a reflex.

When BOTH sides are right — when the oppressor is also a desperate defender, when the rebel's justice requires innocent suffering, when your ally's methods are abhorrent but their cause is righteous — THEN the choice becomes impossible. Players must decide which "right" they value more.

#### How to Implement

**Design Rules:**
- Every faction must have a legitimate grievance or genuine goal that players can understand and potentially sympathize with
- Every antagonist must have a moment where players think "...they have a point"
- Every ally must have a flaw, a compromise, or a cost of association that makes the alliance uncomfortable
- No faction should be "the answer." Allying with anyone requires accepting their flaws
- The strongest ally in the campaign should ALSO be the source of a significant problem (the Illusive Man principle: the entity that helps you most has an agenda that will eventually conflict with yours)

**The Faction Design Test:**
For every faction, answer:
1. What do they want? (Must be defensible)
2. What have they sacrificed for it? (Must be real)
3. What are they willing to do that crosses a line? (Must be uncomfortable)
4. Why can't they coexist with the opposing faction? (Must be structural, not personal)
5. What would the world look like if they won completely? (Must be mixed — good AND bad)

**The NPC Moral Spectrum:**
- Allies should be flawed — selfish, obsessive, pragmatic to the point of cruelty, or kind to the point of naivety
- Enemies should be sympathetic — protecting something, honoring a debt, acting from trauma, or pursuing a genuine good through terrible means
- NO character should be pure. Purity is boring. Purity removes choice.

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — The Illusive Man / Cerberus:**
Cerberus is xenophobic, cruel, and experiments on humans. Cerberus also rebuilt Shepard, funds the suicide mission, and is the ONLY organization willing to act against the Collectors. Working with them is uncomfortable. Not working with them means people die. Both are true simultaneously.

**Mass Effect — The Geth:**
The Geth were created as slaves, fought for their freedom, and just want to be left alone. They also killed billions of Quarians during the Morning War. Are they victims or threats? Both. Always both.

**BG3 — The Emperor:**
Genuinely protects you. Genuinely self-interested. Has killed to survive. Would kill again. Is the only thing between you and ceremorphosis. Is also a mind flayer who consumed someone's identity to exist.

#### D&D Translation

**The Dynasty example (from João's campaign):**
The Kryn Dynasty is simultaneously:
- An oppressor (conquered territory, enforces religious doctrine)
- A desperate defender (fighting existential threats on multiple fronts)
- The party's primary ally (provides resources, sanctuary, information)
- The source of the main problem (their ambition opened the portal they now need help closing)

This is perfect grey morals: the entity you need is also the entity that caused the crisis. Working with them is necessary. Working with them is also enabling the mindset that created the problem.

### TENET 3: THE LIVING WORLD

*Where choices exist and echo — the world has memory, momentum, and scars.*

#### Definition

The campaign world is not a static backdrop waiting for player interaction. It is a living system that:
- **Remembers** — Every choice accumulates. Reputation tracks invisibly. NPCs recall. Factions adjust.
- **Moves** — Factions act on their own timelines. Inaction has cost. Delayed decisions have consequences.
- **Adapts** — Catastrophic errors never end the story. The world reshapes around even the worst outcomes and offers new paths forward — just not the ones players expected.
- **Scars** — The physical landscape reflects what happened. Burned buildings stay burned. Corpses remain. The environment is a RECORD of choices made.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

A living world makes choices MATTER. If the world forgets, choices are meaningless. If the world doesn't move, there's no urgency. If the world can't adapt, failure is a dead end instead of a story. If the world doesn't scar, consequences are invisible.

The living world is what transforms a choice from "a thing that happened in session 12" into "a thing that shaped everything that came after."

#### How to Implement

**Memory — The Invisible Reputation System:**

| Relationship State | How It Manifests (never announced, always demonstrated) |
|---|---|
| Unknown | Neutral treatment. No special access. |
| Noticed | NPCs mention the party to each other. Reputation precedes them slightly. |
| Trusted | Information previously unavailable is volunteered. Doors open. |
| Allied | Active assistance. Resources offered without asking. |
| Wary | Information withheld. Requests treated with suspicion. Prices increase. |
| Hostile | Active opposition. Resources directed against the party. |
| Burned | Permanent. That door is closed forever. |

**Rule:** Never tell players what state they're in. Show it through NPC behavior, access, pricing, and atmosphere.

**Momentum — Consequence Timing:**

| Type | When | How | Purpose |
|------|------|-----|---------|
| Immediate | Same scene | NPC reaction, atmosphere shift, door opens/closes | Confirms the choice mattered RIGHT NOW |
| Short-term | 1-2 sessions | Rumor, letter, NPC mentions it in passing | Keeps the decision present |
| Medium-term | 3-5 sessions | New situation created by the old decision | Reveals the world has its own momentum |
| Long-term | 6-10+ sessions | Former ally transformed, burned bridge now critical | The most devastating — players forgot, the world didn't |
| Finale | The final arc | Everything arrives simultaneously | The definitive audit of what the campaign was |

**Rule:** Plant the seed 3-5 sessions before it blooms. Never announce you're planting it.

**Adaptation — Fail Forward:**
- If the party kills a critical NPC: another NPC fills the role (differently, with new complications)
- If the party fails a mission: the failure creates a NEW situation (not game over — new problems)
- If the party makes a catastrophic error: the world absorbs it and reshapes. There's always a path. Just a harder, stranger, more costly one.

**Rule:** Never block. Always redirect. The story never ends because of failure — it transforms.

**Scars — Environmental Consequences:**
- Buildings destroyed in session 5 are still rubble in session 20
- NPCs killed stay dead — their absence creates new dynamics
- Tavern boards and town criers report on the party's past actions
- Return visits to old locations reveal how they've changed because of what the party did (or didn't do)
- The physical world is a visible TIMELINE of player agency

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — Three-Game Propagation:**
The Rachni Queen in ME1 (spare or kill) → shows up in ME3 either as an ally providing war assets OR as a footnote. A decision made in hour 5 of the trilogy pays off 60+ hours later. The world REMEMBERED for three games.

**BG3 — Micro-Consequences:**
Kill a tiefling in the grove → their partner attacks you 10 minutes later. The world reacted in MINUTES. This immediate feedback confirms: everything you do matters, even small things.

**BG3 — Environmental Reactivity:**
Destroy the grove → in Act 2, you find the survivors scattered, hostile, changed. The landscape of the social world has scarred permanently.

#### D&D Translation

**Prep tool — The Consequence Tracker:**
After every session, note:
1. What did the party choose? (The decision)
2. Who knows? (Which NPCs/factions are aware)
3. When does it echo? (Assign a session number for short/medium/long consequence)
4. How does it manifest? (Specific: a letter, a changed NPC attitude, a closed door, a new enemy)

**The "what if they hadn't" exercise:**
For every major choice, prep ONE sentence of what would have happened if they'd chosen differently. This isn't for the players — it's for YOU, to know the world has real alternative paths that were foreclosed.

### TENET 4: DISCOVERY OVER EXPOSITION

*How the world is understood — lore is earned, not delivered.*

#### Definition

Players learn about the world through FINDING things, not through being TOLD things. Information arrives via:
- **Objects** — Diaries, letters, inscriptions, murals, maps, artifacts
- **Environment** — Architecture, decay, repair, what's present and what's conspicuously absent
- **Behavior** — How NPCs act reveals more than what they say
- **Overheard** — Conversations not directed at the party, rumors, gossip

Information is NEVER delivered through:
- DM monologue / narrator exposition dumps
- NPCs who exist solely to explain things
- Convenient "lore rooms" that exist to catch players up

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

Discovery creates OWNERSHIP. When a player pieces together a truth from scattered clues, that truth belongs to THEM. They feel smart. They're invested. They care about what they learned because they WORKED for it.

Exposition creates passivity. Being told something is forgettable. Finding it is memorable.

Discovery also enables the impossible choice to land harder — because the player who discovered the moral complexity of a situation through environmental clues is more invested than the player who was lectured about it.

#### How to Implement

**The Four Channels of Information Delivery:**

| Channel | What It Delivers | Example |
|---------|-----------------|---------|
| Objects | History, secrets, plot twists | A diary that reveals the "villain" was following orders to protect their family |
| Environment | World state, consequences, atmosphere | A city where one district is rebuilt and another is still rubble — tells you who the government prioritizes |
| Behavior | Character truth, hidden agendas, emotional state | An NPC who flinches when a specific name is mentioned |
| Overheard | Rumors, public perception, faction state | Two guards arguing about whether the party are heroes or criminals |

**Design Rules:**
- For every piece of important lore: decide which channel delivers it BEFORE the session
- Multiple channels reinforcing the same truth > one channel delivering it explicitly
- Players who explore should learn MORE than players who don't — but players who don't should still have a complete game
- The DM never says "you should investigate X." The DM places X in the environment and trusts the players to notice or not

**The Codex Principle (from Mass Effect):**
Optional deep lore should be AVAILABLE for players who seek it, but never MANDATORY. The player who reads every inscription should feel rewarded with deeper understanding. The player who doesn't should never feel lost.

**The Callback Principle (from Mercer):**
Information planted through discovery in session 5 can become the emotional core of session 35 — precisely BECAUSE the player found it themselves and remembers it as THEIR discovery.

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — The Codex:**
Every planet, species, and event has pages of optional lore. You never NEED it. But the player who reads it understands the Genophage choice 10x more deeply because they know the history.

**BG3 — Environmental Lore:**
The Underdark reveals its history through architecture, corpses positioned in telling ways, murals, and the behavior of creatures who've been down there too long. Nobody explains it. You piece it together.

**João's Campaign — Object-Based Plot:**
Plot exposition delivered through inscriptions, diaries, and murals. Players who investigate learn more. The DM never narrates a monologue explaining the political situation — the players discover it through objects and NPC behavior.

#### D&D Translation

**Prep checkpoint:** For every important piece of information in a session, answer:
1. What OBJECT could communicate this? (A letter, a mural, a broken weapon)
2. What ENVIRONMENTAL detail implies it? (The state of a building, who's absent, what's been burned)
3. What NPC BEHAVIOR reveals it? (A flinch, a lie detected, an unexpected kindness)
4. What can be OVERHEARD? (Guards gossiping, merchants complaining, children singing songs about it)

If the answer to all four is "none — I'll just tell them," redesign the information delivery.

### TENET 5: TRUST AS WEAPON

*How impossible choices become devastating — through earned emotional investment.*

#### Definition

The most devastating impossible choices land because the party CARES about what they're choosing between. That caring must be EARNED through genuine positive interaction before being weaponized.

The pattern:
1. **Earn trust** — An NPC, faction, or situation provides genuine help, genuine vulnerability, genuine connection over multiple sessions
2. **Deepen investment** — The relationship grows. The party relies on it. They feel smart for building it.
3. **Weaponize** — The trusted element becomes the mechanism of the impossible choice. The NPC's true agenda conflicts. The faction's methods become indefensible. The alliance requires a betrayal.

The devastating part: the signs were ALWAYS there. In retrospect, the players can trace exactly where the conflict was visible. They chose to trust anyway — because trusting was REASONABLE given what they knew. The betrayal is their failure of judgment, and they'd probably make the same call again.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

Impossible choices between strangers are abstract. Impossible choices between people you LOVE are unbearable. Trust is what transforms a difficult decision into an agonizing one.

If the party doesn't care about both sides of a choice, the choice isn't impossible — it's obvious. Trust-building is what makes both sides matter.

#### How to Implement

**Design Rules:**
- NEVER rush the trust-building phase. Minimum 3-5 sessions of genuine positive interaction before the weaponization
- The NPC/faction MUST provide real help during the trust phase. Not tricks. Not manipulation. Real value.
- The signs of future conflict must be present FROM THE START — but subtle, ignorable, easily rationalized
- When the betrayal/conflict arrives, the NPC's logic must be COMPREHENSIBLE. "I always told you what I was. You chose not to hear it."
- The party should feel: "They're wrong... but I understand why. And I would have done the same thing from their position."

**The Trust Timeline:**

| Phase | Sessions | What Happens | Design Purpose |
|-------|----------|--------------|----------------|
| Introduction | 1 | The NPC/faction appears. First impression is positive or intriguing. | Plant the seed |
| Establishment | 2-4 | They deliver on promises. They're useful. They're likeable. Maybe vulnerable. | Build genuine investment |
| Deepening | 5-8 | The relationship carries real weight. The party relies on them. | Create dependency |
| First Crack | 8-10 | A small moment where their values clash with the party's. Easily dismissed. | Plant the foreshadowing |
| Weaponization | 10+ | Their agenda and the party's become structurally incompatible. The impossible choice arrives. | The payoff |

**The "They Never Lied" Principle:**
The most devastating version of this pattern is when the trusted entity never actually deceived the party. Everything they said was true. Everything they offered was genuine. Their agenda was always visible to anyone paying attention. The betrayal isn't deception — it's structural incompatibility revealed by changing circumstances.

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — The Illusive Man:**
ME2: Funds your mission. Gives you a ship. Brings you back from the dead. Genuinely fights the Collectors. Everything he provides is REAL.
ME3: His methods become monstrous. His goal (control the Reapers) is defensible but his path is indefensible. He never changed. The STAKES changed.

**BG3 — The Emperor:**
Protects you from ceremorphosis. Offers genuine tactical advice. Shares vulnerability about their past. Then asks you to trust them in a situation where trust might mean doom. They never lied. But what they ARE makes the trust terrifying.

**BG3 — Astarion:**
Charming, helpful, flirtatious, vulnerable. Earns your investment over 60+ hours. Then asks for power that might destroy who he's become. You WANT to help him. Helping him might create a monster. The choice is only impossible because you spent 60 hours caring.

#### D&D Translation

**The Long Play:**
TTRPG DMs have an advantage video games don't: TIME. A relationship built over 6 real-world months of weekly sessions carries emotional weight no video game can match. Use this.

**Prep rule:** Every major NPC who will eventually force an impossible choice needs a MINIMUM of 3 sessions of genuine positive interaction with the party before the conflict arrives. There are no shortcuts. You cannot weaponize trust you haven't earned.

**The retrospective test:** After the conflict arrives, players should be able to look back and say: "Oh. That moment in session 4 where they said [X]... that was always there." If they can't — if the betrayal comes from nowhere — you failed at foreshadowing. If they CAN — you succeeded at trust-as-weapon.

### TENET 6: REVELATION THROUGH CRISIS AND REST

*When characters are revealed — both in the fire and in the silence after.*

#### Definition

Characters reveal who they ARE (not what they can DO) in two complementary modes:

**Crisis:** Under pressure, with no time to deliberate, what does a character sacrifice? What do they protect? What line won't they cross? The impossible choice moment strips away rationalization and reveals VALUES.

**Rest:** When the pressure lifts, what do characters process? What do they confess? What can't they let go of? What relationships do they deepen? Downtime reveals the INTERIOR — the thoughts that crisis doesn't leave room for.

Both are essential. Crisis without rest is exhausting noise — constant pressure with no space to feel its weight. Rest without crisis is aimless — there's nothing to process, confess, or reckon with.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

If campaigns are all crisis, players become desensitized. The impossible choice loses weight because there's no space to FEEL it. Players make decisions mechanically because there's no time for emotional engagement.

If campaigns are all rest, nothing matters. Characters have nothing to talk about, no choices to reckon with, no relationships tested by fire.

The rhythm of CRISIS → REST → CRISIS creates the pattern where:
1. Crisis forces a choice
2. Rest allows characters to FEEL the weight of that choice
3. The next crisis is shaped by what happened in rest (confessions lead to new vulnerabilities, deepened relationships create new stakes)

#### How to Implement

**Design Rules:**
- After every major impossible choice: plan a downtime scene where characters can REACT
- Downtime scenes should include: private conversations between PCs, NPC check-ins (how did the choice affect them?), and physical/environmental changes that remind players what happened
- Not every rest scene needs to be dramatic. Sometimes it's just: the party sits in silence around a fire, and that silence means something
- Rest scenes are where SECRETS emerge — things characters weren't ready to say under pressure
- Use rest scenes to PLANT the next impossible choice — a quiet confession creates a new vulnerability the next crisis will test

**The Camp Scene Framework (adapted from BG3):**

After significant events, offer structured downtime:
1. **Private conversations** — Players can approach each other or NPCs one-on-one
2. **Processing** — "How does your character feel about what happened?" (Not a mechanics question — a character question)
3. **Revelation** — Something new emerges. A secret shared. A fear confessed. A decision made quietly.
4. **Foreshadowing** — Something in the rest scene hints at what's coming next

**Pacing Rule:**
- 2-3 sessions of rising tension → 1 session (or portion) of downtime/rest
- Never more than 4 sessions of continuous crisis without a breath
- Never more than 1 full session of pure downtime without tension reentering

#### Examples

**Mass Effect — The Normandy Between Missions:**
Between each mission, Shepard walks the ship. Talks to crew. Learns their fears. These conversations are WHERE the impossible choices of loyalty missions become devastating — because you KNOW these people now.

**BG3 — Camp Scenes:**
After every long rest, companions react. They comment on what you did. They share memories triggered by events. They deepen or withdraw based on your choices. The camp is WHERE you learn who these people are.

**Mercer — The Long Rest in Critical Role:**
Some of the most memorable moments in Critical Role happen in a quiet moment: Caleb confessing to Beau, Vax praying alone, Laudna processing her resurrection. These land because of the crisis that preceded them.

#### D&D Translation

**Ask after pivotal moments:**
- "You're back at camp. The fire is burning. Nobody's talking yet. What does [character] do?"
- "It's been three days since [event]. How has [character] been acting?"
- "In the quiet moment before sleep, [NPC] approaches [character]. What do they say?"

**Don't force it.** Offer the space. Some players will use it. Some won't. The space itself matters.

**The power of silence at the table:**
After an impossible choice is made, let there be a moment of silence before the world reacts. That pause IS the rest. Even three seconds of quiet lets the weight land.

### TENET 7: THE PARTY AS PRESSURE COOKER

*The source of the most personal impossible choices — because the hardest choice isn't "save the world." It's "my friend is wrong and I have to act against them."*

#### Definition

Player character arcs are designed to eventually CONFLICT with each other. Not through character flaws or personal dislike — through structurally incompatible goals, values, or loyalties that become visible as the campaign progresses.

Each PC's backstory intersects the main campaign plot in ways that pull AGAINST other PCs' backstories. The party isn't just a team facing external threats — it's a container of tensions that the campaign slowly pressurizes until something must give.

The table's social dynamics — real human relationships between real players — ARE the deepest consequence system. An NPC disapproving is a mechanic. A friend across the table looking at you with genuine disappointment is something else entirely.

#### Why This Matters for Campaign Design

The most devastating impossible choices aren't "save the village or save the kingdom." They're "my friend's character needs X to survive, but giving them X means my character's family dies."

External threats are abstract. Intra-party tension is PERSONAL. The player isn't choosing between two fictional options — they're choosing between the goals of real people they sit across from every week.

This is where D&D has an advantage no video game can match: the other "companions" are REAL PEOPLE with real emotional investment.

#### How to Implement

**Design Rules:**
- During session zero / character creation: look for STRUCTURAL tensions between PC backstories. If none exist, suggest hooks that create them
- Every PC should have a personal arc that connects to the main campaign — but these connections should PULL IN DIFFERENT DIRECTIONS
- The main campaign should eventually force a moment where helping one PC's arc DAMAGES another's
- Never manufacture false conflict. The tension should emerge organically from the characters' genuine goals and values colliding with the situation
- PCs exert moral pressure on each other naturally — design situations where a choice one PC makes has visible impact on another PC's world

**The Structural Incompatibility Design:**

| PC A wants... | PC B wants... | The Campaign Provides... |
|---|---|---|
| To cure their curse | The curse to be weaponized against the BBEG | A moment where they must choose: cure or weapon |
| To protect their homeland | To expose their homeland's crimes | Information that serves one and threatens the other |
| To honor their dead mentor | To destroy the organization their mentor built | A confrontation with the mentor's legacy |
| To save everyone | To sacrifice the few for the many | A situation where both philosophies are tested simultaneously |

**The Invisible Pressure System:**
- Don't announce "your goals conflict!" — let players DISCOVER it through play
- Let the moment of realization happen at the table organically
- When it does: step back. Let players navigate it. Don't solve it for them.
- The resolution (or lack thereof) IS the campaign's central drama

**Safety Note:**
Intra-party conflict is powerful but dangerous. It requires:
- Players (not just characters) who enjoy dramatic tension
- Clear separation between player and character goals
- A table culture where disagreement is safe
- Lines and veils respected — the conflict is NARRATIVE, never personal
- A DM ready to intervene if the tension becomes harmful rather than dramatic

#### Examples

**BG3 — Companion Conflicts:**
Shadowheart's loyalty to Shar conflicts with Lae'zel's devotion to Vlaakith. Astarion's desire for power conflicts with Karlach's moral clarity. The party can't all get what they want. Someone compromises, or someone leaves.

**Mass Effect — Tali vs. Legion:**
Two squadmates with genuinely incompatible species-level needs. You can broker peace — but only if you've invested heavily in BOTH relationships. Otherwise, you choose.

**Mercer — Caleb and Essek:**
Caleb wants to rewrite history. Essek has committed treason for similar reasons. Their mutual understanding is built on shared guilt — and when push comes to shove, their methods diverge dangerously.

#### D&D Translation

**Session Zero Design:**
When building the party, look for:
1. What does each PC VALUE most? (Family? Freedom? Truth? Power? Duty?)
2. Where do those values potentially CLASH under pressure?
3. What campaign situation would FORCE those values into direct conflict?
4. Is that conflict INTERESTING (produces dramatic tension) or DESTRUCTIVE (produces player frustration)?

**The slow build:**
- Sessions 1-5: PCs are aligned. External threat unites them. Tensions invisible.
- Sessions 6-10: Small disagreements emerge. "I would have done that differently." Easily smoothed over.
- Sessions 10-15: The disagreements become STRUCTURAL. Not personality — GOALS.
- Sessions 15+: The campaign forces the tension to a head. The party must navigate it or fracture.

**The resolution is the story:**
However the party resolves their internal tension — through compromise, sacrifice, one PC yielding, or the group splitting — THAT is what the campaign was about. The external threat is the frame. The internal tension is the painting.

## Mission Structure — The Zone Blueprint

*How tenets manifest at the quest/mission level. Every mission is a self-contained story in a new zone, with at least 2 concurrent plots designed to collide at decision points.*

### PLOT A — Main Mission
The primary objective that serves the overarching campaign narrative. This is WHY the party is in this zone. Clear goal, but execution requires navigating B, C, D, E. Plot A is the spine — everything else wraps around it.

### PLOT B — Secondary Mission (feeds A)
A parallel storyline that helps resolve or advance Plot A — but the CONNECTION between A and B is not obvious at first. Plot B contains:
- A **mystery** behind its relationship to A (players must discover WHY these plots connect)
- A **moral dilemma** with world-level impact (this is where Grey Morals live — Tenet 2)
- The moment players realize B feeds A should feel like a revelation, not an exposition dump (Tenet 4 — Discovery Over Exposition)

### PLOTS C, D, E — Encounters Along the Way
Smaller missions resolved en route to A and B. They serve multiple purposes:
- Provide **clues** that help crack Plot B's mystery
- Give **extra reasons to interact with NPCs** (favors, trades, relationships that feed Tenet 5 — Trust as Weapon)
- Add **world texture** — make the zone feel alive beyond the main quest
- These are where seeds are planted for FUTURE zones (Tenet 3 — The Living World remembers)

### BINARY CHOICES
At the intersection of A and B, force the party into a hard decision:
- **No ideal option** — each has pros AND cons clearly visible before choosing (Tenet 1 — legible cost)
- Both options must feel **defensible** at the table
- The choice should ideally **split the party's opinion** (Tenet 7 — Pressure Cooker)
- The cost is known. The tragedy is choosing anyway.

### CONSEQUENCES (Post-Decision Echoes)
After the party chooses, include **future reminders** that their choice shaped the world:
- Thank-you letters from saved NPCs
- News on a tavern board / town crier about world changes
- NPCs in future zones commenting on the party's reputation
- Environmental changes on return visits to the zone
- Former enemies or allies resurfacing, transformed by the party's choice

**Timing:** Consequences should appear **2-5 sessions later** — delayed enough to feel organic, soon enough to connect back to the decision that caused them.

### How This Connects to the Tenets

| Mission Element | Primary Tenet Served |
|---|---|
| Plot A | Structure — the container for everything else |
| Plot B (mystery + moral dilemma) | Tenet 1 (Impossible Choice) + Tenet 2 (Grey Morals) |
| Plot B's hidden connection to A | Tenet 4 (Discovery Over Exposition) |
| Plots C, D, E (NPC relationships) | Tenet 5 (Trust as Weapon) + Tenet 3 (Living World) |
| Binary Choices | Tenet 1 (Impossible Choice) + Tenet 7 (Pressure Cooker) |
| Consequences | Tenet 3 (The Living World remembers) |
